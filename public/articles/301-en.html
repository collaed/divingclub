🦈 The Maldives: Between Dream and Reality

By Pascale Lucietto, E2 Instructor and PADI Instructor
🌊🐠🦈🐢☀️🏖️🤿🫧

I arrived at the CEP in 1987 after a discovery dive in the Maldives. It was a massive revelation for someone who had only ever swum in pools and done some snorkeling in the Mediterranean. Granted, there are worse places for a first time — but that didn't stop me from getting my N1 and N2 at Lake Haute-Sûre, followed by my N3, N4, and PADI Instructor certification in the Mediterranean.

The idyllic landscapes of the Maldives — white sand beaches and warm turquoise waters — had always made me dream, and diving allowed me to see what was happening beneath the surface. It was there that I first admired manta rays, sharks of all kinds, turtles, barracudas, schools of multicoloured fish, dolphins, vibrant corals, and microscopic creatures that use camouflage so effectively they are impossible to spot with an untrained eye.

From there, a seemingly impossible dream began to take root: to go and work as an instructor on a dive boat in the Maldives. And the dream came true when I left everything behind in Luxembourg to start working in August 1999 as a PADI Instructor on a resort island. During my five and a half years in the Maldives, I worked on several resort islands and spent two eight-month seasons on a liveaboard dive cruise. It was during those years that I logged the vast majority of my thousands of dives. It was also where I learned to work with diving professionals in a profit-driven environment, and to navigate the demands of clients, superiors, and the laws of a 100% Muslim country.

I saw everything one could dream of seeing underwater, and it remains a magnificent memory. But it was also where I discovered the other side of the postcard — working 12 hours a day between discovery dives, pool exercises, theory classes, snorkeling trips, dive supervision, and the necessary socialising with guests. I had to work with all-male teams, European and Japanese colleagues, local Muslim staff, and cheap foreign labour from India, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh. At the time, it was not exactly easy for a European woman on her own, unfamiliar with that world.

If I can offer one piece of advice to all divers: if you want to go diving in the Maldives, go now — before it is too late. The Maldives have changed enormously in 40 years — not because of rising sea levels, since with the help of Dutch engineers and architects they are now building artificial islands everywhere. Mostly luxury hotels, aimed at a new Russian and Asian clientele. The landscape has completely changed: concrete buildings, a gigantic airport, saturated road traffic.

Enormous numbers of tourists come to the Maldives for the mild climate and the diving, but the environment suffers: serious waste management problems (much of it ending up in the sea), pollution, coral bleaching due to El Niño events, big-game and longline fishing — often by the very people who dive to admire the underwater wonders and then catch them to eat. Fishing permits granted to China outside the waters "reserved for tourists," in exchange for aid such as the construction of the Friendship Bridge connecting Malé to the airport and a second hospital.

In recent years, a new practice has developed: baiting large sharks by dropping dead fish into the water, so that divers can swim among them — mainly tiger sharks that have gathered around an atoll island, attracted by the smell of rotting fish. The goal is always the same: to get rich at the expense of the environment and the respect owed to this magnificent, exuberant nature.

A sad observation, but a realistic one. I am aware that by continuing to dive in the Maldives several times a year aboard a liveaboard, I am contributing to my carbon footprint, enriching people who are not necessarily passionate about diving, and polluting despite myself. But I love this country so much, and as long as there are so many beautiful things to see, I hope to keep diving there.

Good to know:

  • Water temperature: 27–30°C year-round (unfortunately too warm for the corals)
  • Best season: January to April (though seasons are increasingly disrupted by climate change)
  • You can dive year-round, with the risk of heavy rain and wind
  • Maximum depth: 30 metres — this is not a PADI standard but a law of the country
  • Often current (light to very strong) — N2 with experience recommended
  • SMB (surface marker buoy) skills essential for the drift diving techniques specific to the Maldives

If you are interested, I would be delighted to give you all the information you need. And who knows — perhaps we will head out together for some magnificent dives.
